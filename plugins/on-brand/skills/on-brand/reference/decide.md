# Decide: the questions only the person can answer

Extraction shows how the brand looks today. It can't tell you what each color is for, what the brand would never do, or why. Those answers are what keep AI-made work from drifting back to generic, so this step matters most.

## How to ask

- **One question at a time.** Wait for the answer before the next one.
- **Frame it, then suggest.** Open every question with where you are and what it's for, before any suggestion: "Question 3 of 10: type. This picks the fonts for headlines and for everything else, and it shows up on every post and page." Then lead with a suggestion drawn from what `pull` found, so they react instead of starting cold: "Your buttons and links use the coral. Should coral be the one accent, used only for things people click?" Without the frame, people lose track of which question they're answering and how far along they are.
- **Take answers in whatever order they come.** People often answer two questions at once ("I love the blues, but nothing loud" while talking about their audience). Note it, skip what's already answered, and only ask what's still open. Keep the numbering honest: if a question got answered early, say so ("Question 3 of 10: you've already covered colors, so on to type").
- **Show anything visual instead of describing it.** Corners, shadows, spacing, motion and font choices land when people can see them. Where you can show HTML or images, make a small side-by-side in their colors (three corner styles on the same card, three fonts on the same headline) and ask them to pick. Use the practice piece from `pull` as the card where you can, so every choice is seen on something they'll really make. Where you can't show anything, describe each option by what it looks like, not its design name. People often pick something other than your recommendation once they see the options.
- **Note reactions one detail at a time.** "I like it" or "not that one" usually means part of it. Ask which part, and note each detail with what they said: what to keep, and any objection to how it was done ("likes the blue, wants it darker"; "likes the round shapes, but these feel too cute"). A detail they like can survive an option they reject. Carry these notes into the summary.
- **Check every suggestion against the pull before you offer it.** People accept most suggestions as written, so a wrong suggestion becomes a wrong rule. If a suggestion goes against something the pull found, say so in the question: "Your site uses a hard offset shadow on the headshot. Keep it, or should shadows be on the never-list?" Never suggest a rule the evidence contradicts without naming the evidence.
- **Plain words, and describe what things look like.** Say "the color for headlines", not "primary token"; "how rounded the corners are", not "border radius". Many people who care about design aren't designers, and design words get misread. When a word has more than one meaning, describe each one: "a soft, blurry shadow under a card" vs. "a solid block offset behind it, with no blur"; "art that runs off the edge of the page" instead of "bleed".
- **Accept short answers.** "Yes", "not that", "whatever you think" are fine. For "whatever you think", make the call, say what you chose and why, and move on. "Skip" is always an answer too: use a sensible default, say what it is in a few words, and note it in the summary so they can change it later.
- **Ten questions at most.** Skip any question the pull already answered with confidence; just confirm it in the summary.
- If there was little to pull (no site, no guide), ask the same questions with examples instead of suggestions, and offer two or three concrete options for colors and fonts. If there was almost nothing, use "Starting from scratch" below first.

## Starting from scratch

Some people arrive with no site, no pieces and only a rough idea. Ten questions answered cold won't get far for them; they need things to react to. Tell them up front that this takes longer than half an hour, then:

1. **Gather things to react to.** Ask for three to five things they like the look of, of any kind: a shop sign, a book cover, a café, a website, a photo. For each, say back what you see in it (the colors, the type, the shapes, the mood) and ask which parts they like. If they have nothing, show three or four named looks as small samples and ask the same thing. Note reactions one detail at a time (see "How to ask").
2. **Two or three directions on the practice piece.** Make the practice piece in each direction, with the same words, so only the look changes. Use real fonts and their colors from step 1. Each direction gets a short name and one line on what it's built from.
3. **Pick one, then refine it.** Revise from the one they picked, one change at a time (see "Revise from the version they picked" in SKILL.md). Mixing in a detail from another direction is fine when they ask for it.
4. **Try it on a second kind of piece** (a Story, a flyer, an email header). If it feels disconnected, check size, alignment and spacing before reopening the colors or fonts.
5. **Then the questions.** Most of questions 2 to 4 are now answered on screen; confirm them in a line each and ask the rest.

**What's out of scope:** designing a logo, custom illustration, and motion sequences. They're real design work and can take many rounds. Set the business name in the chosen headline font as a stand-in wordmark, labeled as a stand-in, and list a logo, illustration and motion as open decisions in the summary, with one line saying a designer or a separate session can take them on.

**A guide can be the finish.** If they'd rather stop with a clear direction and take it to a designer or to Canva, that's a good outcome: write the files, make the visual guide (see `reference/write.md`) and skip installing the skill unless they want it.

## The questions

Ask in this order. The wording is a guide; adjust it to the person and what you found.

1. **Point of view.** "Who are you making things for, and what should they feel in the first three seconds of seeing one of your posts?" Suggest a main audience and a second one if the evidence points to both. Then ask for the story behind the name: "Where does the name come from?" A name often holds a motif the brand can use (an animal, a place, a habit) that no file will show.
2. **Colors and their jobs.** Confirm every color found, including tints like border shades, hover colors and muted text: "These four lighter shades show up on borders and small text. Are they part of the system?" Then: "Which one should do most of the work, which is the background, and which are the accents that only show up for important things?" A brand can have one or two accents if each has its own job. Get a rough share for each, like mostly warm white, navy for text, coral rarely. Ask: "Do you have names for these colors?" and use their names in the files.
   - **No pop color.** Some palettes are calm on purpose: all one hue, nothing loud. Don't push an accent on them. Ask instead: "When one thing has to stand out, like a date or a sale, how should it stand out?" The answer is often a solid block of the darkest color with light text, a bigger size, or the brand's mark around it.
   - **A loved color that fails as text** can stay. Say what it can do: fills, backgrounds, shapes, dividers and the logo, with a darker color for any words on it. Don't steer them into dropping it.
3. **Type.** "One font for headlines and one for everything else. Here's what your site uses. Keep them, or is there a reason they don't feel right?" With no site, show two or three pairings on the same headline in their colors and let them pick. Note why the fonts fit, in their words.
   - **Show real fonts, set as live text.** Load each candidate from its real file (Google Fonts, `@fontsource`, or a file they own) and set it on the same words at the same size. Label each with its family, weight and source. Never offer lettering drawn by an image model as a font choice: it can't be installed, typed or matched later.
   - **Show the whole set, not one word.** A headline font gets chosen alongside the body font, a subhead and a small label, on the practice piece. Say in a line why each pairing works (contrast in shape, matching proportions, a shared mood), so they can judge it.
4. **Shape, space and motion.** Four separate looks, each shown rather than named (see "How to ask"): how rounded the corners are; how much room there is (lots of breathing room, or packed with information); shadows (none, a soft blurry one, or a solid offset block with no blur); and motion. Ask about motion through what they make: "Do you make any Reels, Stories or GIFs, or plan to?" If not, write "still" and move on.
5. **Refusals.** "What would never appear in your brand? Colors, styles, words, kinds of photos, anything." Push gently for specifics: "Anything you've seen a competitor do that you'd never do?" This is the most important question. Aim for at least five items. Check each suggested item against the pull (see "How to ask"), and against how the business works: "no other companies' logos in photos" is right for some businesses and wrong for one that sells or features other companies' products.
6. **Assets.** "Do you have a logo file, photos you own, illustrations or patterns we should use? Where do you keep them? Anything we should never use, like an old logo?" If the pull found an illustration folder, show it and ask whether it's the full set. Ask for **the story behind the logo** too: "Is there a meaning in the logo, like a shape or a mark?" A detail that reads as decoration (a ring, a line, a squiggle) can turn out to be the brand's signature, and that changes how it's used.
   - **Where the logo or mark goes** is easier to settle on a real piece than in the interview. Write a starting rule ("the mark once per piece, bottom right"), and expect it to change when they see the test piece. In an editable piece, offer it as a choice (logo, mark, none) so it stays open.
7. **Reasoning.** "When something you've made looks off, what's usually the reason?" (Too busy, wrong color, looks cheap, looks like everyone else, nothing lines up.) The answer becomes a rule.
8. **Where it shows up.** "What do you make most often: social posts or banners, email newsletters, slides, PDFs like proposals or guides, flyers, web pages?" This decides which format files get written, and which platforms to note in the social file. Default to social posts and documents if they're unsure. Note anything they want to make later, and say plainly that those get a line in the system now and their own rules the first time they're made. Don't promise a file for them.
   Then ask straight away: **"Do these rules hold for everything you make, or are there exceptions?"** For example, slides that are louder than the website, photos allowed in decks but not on the site, a color that fills whole slides but only appears in small doses on posts. Write exceptions into the matching format file.
   Two short follow-ups, in the same question:
   - **"Will you print anything?"** (Flyers, signs, bags, stickers, business cards.) If yes, the documents file gets print values: RGB, plus CMYK marked as a starting point to confirm with the printer, and a note to ask the printer for a Pantone match for anything printed in volume.
   - **"When I make something for you, how do you want it?"** Offer the choices as a short list:
     1. A finished image or PDF, ready to post or print
     2. An editable page, where they change the words, pick between their brand's color pairings and sizes, and download the image
     3. A PowerPoint or Word file
     4. A file to finish in Canva or Adobe (a PDF or PowerPoint they upload there, where it opens as an editable design)
     5. Whatever fits each piece

     They can pick more than one ("images for posts, PowerPoint for decks"). The answer sets two lines in their SKILL.md: `Delivery:` in their words, and `Edit mode:` (`always` if they picked the editable page, `ask` for "whatever fits", otherwise `never`). If they'll use the editable page, it gets built during setup (see `reference/write.md`). If they work in Canva or Adobe, the handover gives them a kit for it (see "The handover" in `reference/make.md`).
9. **Words on the page.** "How much text should go on an image? For example, a headline of six words or fewer and no more than four or five short phrases. What goes on the graphic, and what goes in the caption? Sentence case or title case for headlines?"
10. **Copy habits.** "Any words, punctuation or habits that should never show up? Exclamation points, emoji, ALL CAPS, certain buzzwords? How is your business name written? How do you write your web address, email and social handles on a piece (for example, in capitals)? How do you write prices, dates, times and opening hours ("10 am to 6 pm" or "10–6"), and calls to action?"

If they're stuck on question 1 or 5 (what it should feel like, what it would never do), that's normal. Suggest they look at work they like first, and point them to Lucie's public list of places to look: https://hijinx-studio.notion.site/design-resources. Offer to carry on with the other questions and come back.

Voice gets one line in PRODUCT.md (three words for how the brand sounds). If they want more than that, suggest a separate voice skill.

## Name the style

Most people can't name their style, and a name helps every tool that reads the files later. After question 7, offer two or three named styles that fit what you've seen, plus one look-alike to rule out, and let them react: "Your work reads as Swiss (grid, clean sans serif, lots of white space) with a terminal nod. The look-alike to avoid is vaporwave, which also uses classical busts and purple." Use names designers and AI tools already know. If none fits, describe it in a short phrase of their own ("terminal meets gallery"). Write the name into DESIGN.md's overview and the look-alike into Do's and Don'ts. For a longer list of style names, point to a reference like "50 Design Styles Every Designer Should Know for Better Prompting" (UX Planet), without copying from it.

## The summary

Before writing anything, show one screen that covers every decision, grouped: who it's for, colors with jobs and shares, type, shape and space, never do, assets, where it shows up (and any exceptions by output), copy rules, how pieces get delivered. Mark any contrast pair that fails. Where reactions came in detail by detail, list what to keep and what to drop ("keep: the round shapes; drop: anything cute").

**Show it, too.** Next to the summary, where you can render HTML, show a small sample with the decisions applied: their practice piece (or a mini post) with their headline font, body font, colors in their jobs, their corner style, a tag or label, and their logo or mark. People catch a wrong color or font on a real piece that they'd read past in a list, and it costs far less to fix now than after five files are written. If they adjust ("darker blue", "less cream"), change it and show it again.

End with a short list of what's still open: values that are estimated, habits seen only once, anything they said they'd decide later. List only real open items. If nothing is estimated, leave the line out: "Still estimated: none of the colors" reads as "the colors aren't exact."

End with: "Anything to change before I write your files?" Only move on to `reference/write.md` after a yes or their changes.
